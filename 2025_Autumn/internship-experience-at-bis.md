My internship at the Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS), Coimbatore, was not just about rules, regulations, and quality checks; it was also about experiences, realisations, and a few laughs along the way.

- - -

### First Impressions

On my very first day, when I walked into the BIS office, I was mentally prepared for a corporate-like setup, glass doors, shiny floors, and cubicles buzzing with laptops. Instead, I found myself in a setting that looked every bit like a classic _sarkari daftar_. File stacks, calm corridors, and that unmistakable government-officish vibe, it caught me by surprise. For a moment, I thought, “_mai yha kese work karungi_”. It set the tone perfectly: this was going to be a very different kind of journey.

- - - 

### How I Got Into BIS

The internship at the Bureau of Indian Standards was offered through our on-campus drive in March. The application process was simple; we had to submit the required documents in the prescribed format via a Google form. From these applications, students were shortlisted on the basis of CGPA, and two students were selected from the department.

- - - 

### What My Internship Was About

Under the guidance of **Mr. Rino John (Scientist-D, BIS)**, my project revolved around understanding how national standards are implemented and where gaps exist between policy and practice. I worked on:

- **Gap analysis** of uPVC pipe manufacturing across a large-scale industry (Supreme Industries) and an MSME (Flow Tech Power).
- **Pre-standardisation studies** on multilayer water tanks and Methyl Ethyl Ketone (MEK), two products without dedicated Indian Standards.
- **Retail-level compliance survey** of bicycle reflectors under the Quality Control Order, 2023.
- A **laboratory visit** at SROL, Chennai, where I got to see high-end instruments like FTIR, ICP-MS, and GC-MS in action.

- - -

### Why Indian Standards Matter?

Before my internship, “_Indian Standards_” for me were just some codes written on product labels like IS 4985, IS 12701, and so on. But during these two months, I understood their real value. Standards are not just checkboxes; they are what make sure the water tank in your house is safe, the pipe in your farm won’t burst, and even the reflector on your cycle can save your life on a dark road.

In the industries, I saw how strict compliance with IS codes ensured quality and safety, while gaps in implementation can lead to inconsistencies and risks. In fact, my project showed me that while large industries follow standards with advanced systems, MSMEs sometimes struggle due to a lack of resources. That’s where BIS steps in, not just to enforce rules, but to support industries in meeting them.

What struck me most was this: **_standards are invisible, but their impact is everywhere._** From the food we eat to the products we use daily, Indian Standards quietly ensure trust, safety, and uniformity.

As a Chemical Engineering student, I spent a lot of hours reading about chemical reactions, heat transfer, and fluid flow in thick textbooks, solving equations on paper. But during my BIS internship, when I stepped inside factories, those very concepts stood in front of me, not just as formulas, but as giant machines, pipes, distillation towers, and testing labs.

Suddenly, the word “heat exchanger” was no longer just a diagram but rather a roaring piece of equipment that cooled tons of fluid every hour. “_Reaction kinetics_” wasn’t just symbols on a page; it was reactors bubbling inside a petrochemical plant. Even something as simple as fluid flow, which we solve with Reynolds numbers in class, looked so different when I watched PVC melt flow inside extruders to become sturdy pipes.

Over **_eight industry visits and one lab visit_** later, I realized that engineering is not about writing derivations in exams; it’s about making sure processes run safely, efficiently, and within standards. That experience gave me a new respect for what we study in classrooms: those concepts truly form the backbone of industry.

- - -

### The Challenges

One big takeaway was seeing how differently large industries and MSMEs operate. Big players had automated systems, renewable energy usage, and Six Sigma dashboards, while smaller units ran on manual methods, local innovations, and jugaad. Both had their strengths and struggles, but the contrast was eye-opening.

- - -

### Memorable Moments

Some of my most memorable moments came not from the desk work but from the field. Whether it was shopkeepers being blissfully unaware of BIS rules for bicycles or industry engineers passionately explaining why their process was “_the best_,” every conversation added colour to my internship.

### Lessons I Carry Forward

- **Standards are invisible but Powerful.** They protect consumers and shape industries.
- **Fieldwork > Theory.** Visiting factories and labs taught me more than any manual could.
- **Flexibility is Key.** From offices to industries to retail shops, every environment demanded a different lens of observation.

### Advice for Juniors

If you ever intern at an organisation like BIS, go with an open mind. It’s not a corporate internship; it’s a mix of technical exposure, field learning, and people skills. Don’t underestimate the value of every industry visit or shop survey; that’s where the real learning hides.

In short, my BIS internship taught me that standards are not just documents; they are the invisible threads holding our industries, products, and safety together. And yes, it also taught me that government offices may not look corporate, but they sure know how to shape the backbone of the nation.
