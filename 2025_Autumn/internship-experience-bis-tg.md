When I first walked into the Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS) headquarters in Delhi, I honestly didn’t know what to expect. To many, BIS is just another government body—but to me, during my internship, it became the place where I realized how **invisible standards quietly protect millions of lives.**

I was part of the Water Resources Department, working on two fascinating areas: **dam break analysis** and **small hydropower planning.** On paper, that might sound technical, but to me, it was a journey of discovery. I still remember sitting at my desk, asking myself a daunting question: *"What leads to the failure of dams?"* Each day, I tried to untangle this puzzle—studying breach widths, peak outflows, and international methodologies, then figuring out how they could be adapted to India’s unique context. It was humbling to realize that even my small contribution might one day find its way into a **national safety standard.**

The most exciting part of my internship was undoubtedly the **site visits.** Until then, my work had been limited to research papers, codes, and data. But the moment I stepped into the field, everything shifted.

At **Galogi SHP near Mussoorie** and **Pathri SHP in Roorkee**, I saw how small hydropower plants are planned and operated in challenging terrains. Standing near the flowing water channels, I observed not just the physical structures but also the decision-making behind them—why a particular site was chosen, what risks it carried, and how safety considerations were built into the design.

One of the most fascinating aspects was exploring **different types of turbines.** I learned how their operation varies with head and discharge, and the strategies used to decide which turbine best suits a project. Seeing Kaplan, Francis, and Pelton turbines in action—and understanding why one works better in a certain setting than another—was like watching theory transform into reality.

My visit to **IIT Roorkee** revealed the academic side of hydropower development—how experiments, simulations, and prototypes help validate ideas before they are scaled up. It deepened my understanding of how research directly contributes to safer and more efficient designs.

The structures we had studied on paper suddenly came alive—the roar of water, the scale of the gates, the precision of the engineering. In those moments, abstract codes and equations transformed into tangible realities. More importantly, I understood that safety is not just about numbers and designs—it is about unseen layers of protection that people only realize when they are absent.

Looking back, BIS gave me much more than professional exposure—it gave me perspective. It taught me patience when data was incomplete, persistence when methods seemed too complex, and pride in being part of something larger than myself. Most importantly, it reinforced my conviction that as engineers, we don’t just design structures—we design safety, trust, and resilience for society. Safety, I realized, is like salt in food—you may not notice it when it is present, but the moment it is missing, its importance becomes undeniable.
